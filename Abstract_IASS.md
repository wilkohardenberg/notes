### A history of sea level: from geodetic vertical datum to marker of anthropogenic change.

The level of the sea has become a trope of environmental discourse, whose rise is used to symbolise current and future changes to the natural framework that surrounds us. In daily news accounts mean sea level in particular is treated as a natural, clearly identifiable element, an unavoidable baseline for our current apprehensions of anthropogenic global warming. The scientific and technological means used to measure and interpret these changes have a history that spans, however, a much longer period than that covered by the actual awareness of the role of humans in environmental change.

In this talk I discuss the history of sea levels as social constructs and look at how they became part of the nineteenth century movement towards a new understanding of the environment through its transformation in discrete, more legible features. My aim is to use the case of mean sea level to question from where ubiquitous elements of analysis like this come, what purpose they serve, and if they still correspond to current needs.

#### Short CV
Wilko Graf von Hardenberg is senior research scholar at the Max Planck Institute for the History of Science in Berlin where he coordinates the research cluster Art of Judgement and works on a history of the concept of sea level and its shifting materiality. Trained as a political historian and a geographer in Turin, Bruxelles, and Cambridge his research activities focus on the intersection between history of science and technology and environmental history, paying in particular attention to issue relating to environmental change, socio-ecological conflicts, and nature conservation. Prior to moving to Berlin he worked at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, the Rachel Carson Center for Environment and Society and the Deutsches Museum in Munich, the University of Trento, and the Scuola Normale Superiore in Pisa.
