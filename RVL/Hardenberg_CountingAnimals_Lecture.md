Last week Christian Kassung has led us over a fascinating walk through the industrialisation of meat production around 1900, on both a highly theoretical and a more material level. Nature conservation and industrialisation are often rethorically presented as polar opposites. Truth be told, there would be no conservation without the pressures of industrialisation. They are two sides of the same medal. As we will discuss today also many methods of conservation derive in a way from the methods by which industrial growth was organised: bookkeeping and statistics. 

Conservation almost always involves nostalgic claims about the past—along with calls to return to a past prior to the industrial transformation of the world or recapture some aspect of it. Activists, scholars, and practitioners often invoke images of historical abundance and subsequent decline in their pleas to preserve what is left of wild nature, and they use these images to promote programs that aim to return ecosystems to their natural, or “original,” conditions. On occasion these images favour one species in respect to others, especially when facing alien or invasive species. Such calls span the diversity of environmental discourse— from the conservation of endangered species, to the restoration of ecosystems, to the re-wilding of entire landscapes and even continents. We must protect or restore species, ecosystems, and landscapes, the argument goes, because at some time in the past the world was so much more productive and diverse.

All animal species conservation and management efforts are based, in a way or the other, on the need to count animals. The number of animals is, for instance, the most immediare indicator of the viability of a population, on any scale: from the extremely local to the global. The IUCN Red List (SLIDE IUCN) for instance gives estimates of population as an immediate indicator of a species risk status. Moreover it indicates the trend the population is following. To produce the latter some sort of baseline is needed, a marker of past populations that may allow to determine how the population is faring.  

The concept of baselines, first developed in 18th century geodesy and broadened throughout the 19th and early 20th century to generally mean a particularly significant reference framework, was fully embedded into conservation biology only around the 1960s. This does however not mean that there was no need or perception of reference points in earlier attempts at enacting nature conservation. The idea that to study and preserve nature certain starting conditions needed to be formally recorded is much older than that, older even than the discipline of ecology itself. From its very beginning conservation needed thus some sort of “baselines”, even if they weren’t yet called that, to frame what and how to preserve. This idea took hold rapidly. In 1930 the Italian botanist Giovanni Negri suggested that Italy’s national parks should act as research stations aimed at understanding how nature affects the ‘pristine conditions’ if left at its own course. Here he built on a claim he had already made in 1921: only the conservation of all environmental conditions, possible only in national parks, would allow to preserve for further research ecosystems subjected to radical changes due to human intervention.

Beside the professed pristine conditions and singular characteristics of different regions, occasionally the perceived dwindling number of one or the other animal species was called upon to justify proposals to set up a variety of national parks and nature reserves. Today I will touch upon a few examples addressing the reconstruction of past populations—discussing the social construction of past animal population baselines in the US—, the practice of land based surveys within a smallish European national park, and the links between animal surveying and (post)colonial politics in the vast expanses of an African national park.

Consider now the following questions: how many wolves used to roam the lower US 48 states , and how much suitable habitat would be needed to restore the species to its former range (SLIDE WOLF? This question involves problems of time in that it demands some reckoning with the past—a backwardlooking attempt to reconcile conservation and restoration goals with environmental conditions as they once were. Moreover it involves problems of space, requiring us to consider the scale
of our restoration ambitions.

Yet the use of history as a guide to conservation programs is fraught with challenges. On a methodological level,
conservationists too often adopt historical baseline data uncritically, using early accounts of wildlife numbers without closely examining the manner in which these accounts were recorded, transmitted, and interpreted by past generations. Such accounts often involved a high degree of guesswork and extrapolation, a problem compounded by the fragmentary nature of documentary and oral records, which calls into question the idea that the past is knowable and accessible to the contemporary observer. Frequently rough proxies were used, such as the amount of perceived "damage" certain species produced as an indicator of population density. This was subject to conflict, with different actors presenting different numbers.

Take the example of another predator, the bear, in the Italian Alps. Their numbers have been dwindling throughout the 20th c., reaching just three animals by the end of the century. This was unsustainably low population and led to repopulation efforts (SLIDE BEAR). The amount of damage was often inflated, especially by herders and farmers, as a way to obtain monetary compensation. Their numbers were consequently also inflated. Another method used to estimate their density was to count how many heads were hunted. This had the obvious setback that it did not consider the impact of the hunts themselves on the numbers.

The use of historical ecological data as a contemporary reference point has also been challenged on the grounds that ecosystems are dynamic, fluid, and always changing, which makes it difficult to identify a single moment in time as the goal for contemporary conservation programs.

There have been few attempts to document the history of efforts to establish ecological baselines for science and conservation, or the challenges that managers have encountered when they have tried to use baseline historical data for restoration at multiple scales of biological organization.

In North America, early efforts to define animal population baselines, began with maps and censuses that attempted to inventory a continent many European newcomers believed was not substantially or permanently altered by indigenous human activities. For the naturalists and explorers who explored the continent during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, however, North America was a primeval wilderness that contained its entire cast of native species. To map and count the populations of these species was, they thought, to view nature in its raw, primeval state.

During the late nineteenth century, naturalists estimated the populations of some of the America's most conspicuous game species. Their efforts were motivated in part by scientific interest, but even more by a desire to quantify the spectacular declines of charismatic native species, such as the plains bison. Authors would usually begin with the accounts of earlier explorers and surveyors. They would then combine this information with contemporary indicators, such as the population densities of the domestic and feral livestock that had replaced native species on the range. This would render a crude estimate of “carrying capacity.” This number would then be extrapolated to cover the wild species’ presumed historical range. It is using this method that Seton calculated a pre-contact bison population of 65 million animals. 

The fragmentary nature of the sources behind these calculations required however to make many assumptions to get to this result. Early explorers’ accounts were ambiguous and impressionistic, and they were often crafted more to serve political or institutional objectives than to provide rigorous documentation of ecological phenomena. It is unclear what contemporary populations of domestic species could reveal about past populations of wild species. Range maps often relied on conjecture, and
they failed to account for differences in population densities across the species’ range. Seton’s approach ignored indigenous human activities, including changes in those activities over time such as the adoption of guns and horses. It also assumed that “historic conditions” were static, timeless, and deterministic, rather than dynamic, changeable, and contingent, which is the view embraced by most scholars today. Indeed, in some cases, the wildlife populations documented by settlers and explorers may have been unusually large, due to declines in indigenous hunting and gathering during the preceding decades. Despite these shortcomings, nineteenth century estimates of past animal populations profoundly shaped the belief that, before European contact, North America was a place of almost unimaginable wildlife diversity and abundance.

The area of the Gran Paradiso (GP MAP) massif in northwestern Italy has historically hosted the last surviving colony of Alpine ibex (Capra ibex), from which all current populations in the Alps originate. Authorities were aware of the importance of conserving this remnant population since the early nineteenth century. In 1821, King Carlo Felice issued a total ban on hunting ibex and, in
the 1850s, King Vittorio Emanuele II set up a royal hunting reserve in the region with its own dedicated corps of wardens whose mission was to preserve the endangered game. In 1919, as part of a wider attempt to divest himself of royal properties for economic reasons in the difficult years following World War I, his grandson King Vittorio Emanuele III donated the reserve to the state, with the caveat that it had to become a national park. This led, after some debate, to the establishment of the Gran Paradiso National Park in late 1922, just a couple of months after Mussolini’s Fascist movement ascended to power. Even though formally instituted by a coalition government led by the Fascist party, the park was essentially a product of political debates originating in the previous Liberal era. This was reflected in the autonomy granted to the park in its first decade of existence. 

In the report of the commission set up by the Italian ministry of agriculture in 1920 to discuss the feasibility of the institution of a national park in the former royal hunting reserve on the Gran Paradiso massif recently donated by the king to the state, botanist Lino Vaccari addressed the fact that by then it had already been long that the increasing risk for animal and vegetal species to disappear was lamented: “much [I quote] of what lives, thus, too much of what has scientific, historical or aesthetic value is disappearing, and for a sad irony of destiny disappears because it is overcome inexorably by the needs of civil progress” [end of quote]. Following hereupon Vaccari denounces that many new scientific discoveries would become impossible if the environments in which first observations could be made were destroyed. The baselines of early conservationism were something that was thought as something that could be preserved in their own right in specific locales. The aim of the new parks and reserves ought to be just to preserve physically a variety of exemplary **natures**: conservation was presented as offering nature the opportunity to express itself undisturbed and recover, in some select regions of the country, from human interventions. 

Discussing the viability of the selected area to set up a national park the commission claimed that it would have been hard to find elsewhere a scientifically more relevant region than the one of the Gran Paradiso massif. The variety of local ecosystemic features promised to allow scientists a variety of research activities in many different fields. The ibex in particular, “one of the few survivors of the fauna that saw the birth of man happening,” seemed to deserve protection for its gravely endangered state. According to the report in 1919 the ibex stock numbered (DIAGRAMMI) 2493 individuals and the chamois, another endemic species, 1436. In both cases other smaller colonies survived outside the former hunting reserve. Including smaller colonies surviving right outside the former royal hunting reserve there were thus possibly 3000 ibex in the whole region at the end of World War Two. The plans for the institution of a park required evidently an assessment of the existing state of things. The baseline was here formalized on paper mainly because of the needs of the legal handover from the Royal House to state administration. 

According to the annual ibex censuses, conservation in the park was initially a success. Various sources show that the
number of ibex in the region increased from 2370 in 1922 to 3865 in 1933. In 1933, Benito Mussolini’s government, as part of its move since 1925 towards the thorough centralization of the state and the implementation of totalitarianism at all levels, disbanded the park’s autonomous administration, steeped in the ideals of pre-Fascist liberal conservationism, and put the Fascist forestry corps Milizia Nazionale Forestale in charge. Under the direct rule of the Milizia, through the difficult years of World War II and its aftermath, the ibex population dwindled. In 1942 there were just 1564 ibex left in the park and, in 1945, only 419. This sudden turn in the population trend in the years following 1933 has been customarily attributed to an increase in poaching due to the poor quality of surveillance provided by the Milizia’s unmotivated and inexperienced foresters, in contrast to that granted by the local wardens the park had hired in its first decade of existence. Anthropic variables have thus been at the core of the narrative of both the park’s successes and pitfalls: the life of a preserved species has been depicted as being completely dependent, for better or worse, on either the positive impact of conservation policies or the role of mismanagement, which
enabled poaching.

The main problems that arise in applying statistical methods to the long-term history of animal populations are the dearth of data, the limitations of their reliability, and the recurring need to look for viable proxies. This is the case not only when all data are estimates, such as was the case for the bison of the NA plains, but also when data were recorded fairly regularly. Because of the varying relevance of certain facts in respect to others, different regimes and social structures produce, in fact, different data. Gathering, collecting, and ordering data are always political acts. They are undertaken for specific aims and purposes that may differ radically from those of current practitioners and may be influenced by extra-scientific factors. Moreover, the quality of bureaucratic record-keeping and data preservation varies over time. The combination of these aspects greatly restricts our ability to compare datasets throughout history: once data produced by different regimes are merged for long-term comparison, they end up being “artifacts driven by present concerns” rather than internally coherent series. Similar discourses can obviously be made about other kinds of historical data, such as economic and demographic series, but the problem seems particularly relevant in the field of population ecology because of the limited availability of continuous and reliable series of animal population data in historical times.

In this regard the case of the Gran Paradiso National Park, while definitely not unique, may be exceptional, as its long conservation history and the focus on just one iconic species has produced a relatively consistent and extensive data series. On the other hand, as is often the case with protected areas, it is not possible to apply commonly-adopted alternative methods to make assumptions about animal populations, such as estimating them on the basis of their ranges, since the censuses, if not
the actual population, were limited to the park. What also is lacking are data that have the degree of detail common in more recent surveys (e.g., detailed subdivisions by sex or age classes), which might allow more advanced and fine-grained models to be adopted. Additional data provided by a Milizia officer, who recorded a major decrease in birth rates and a worrying flattening of the sex ratio in the years immediately after the population decline began, cover too short a period or are too anecdotal to be of much further use. Both might, however, hint towards additional ecological causes for the decline in the 1930s. Historical data about other species in the region, such as the chamois, are unfortunately only recorded sketchily. Had they been available they could have helped to support possible narratives about interspecies competition with descriptive models.

Animal populations are often modeled, to give practitioners insights in future trends and explain past events. such as sudden population increases or decreases. Data based models, while quite powerful, are, however, “always contingent on the kind of evidence available.” We cannot ask the data at hand to reveal any information they do not contain. Historically produced data often are unable to offer the necessary levels of reliability and precision. 

Can the data gathered during the Fascist regime be accepted as a reliable account of the actual size of the animal population, or are there any clues that they might be somehow skewed? As by-products of specific regimes of fact production, animal censuses, both historical and current, may be biased in many ways: the motives for which they are taken, the methods and tools used to take them, and the training and expertise of those who take them may all affect the reliability of the data. Even uninterrupted series or extremely recent datasets may hide behind an appearance of stability major shifts in procedure, effectiveness, and observational techniques.

Animal censuses are extremely labor-intensive, a fact that may further influence the trustworthiness of the count. For one thing, humans will necessarily have to go where the animals are, possibly disturbing them and thus distorting the results. The details of the stories brhind data production are often lost because of missing accounts. They can still be reconstructed, at least in part, by bearing in mind the broader social and political context at the time they were collected.

Various doubts may arise about the available interwar ibex censuses fro Gran Paradiso. After WWI wardens adopted a ground count method, conducting ibex population censuses twice a year. Since then they follow established routes and search visually, with the aid of binoculars, for ibex within their habitat. The reliability of these surveys still needs to be evaluated in detail. Nonetheless, it has been suggested “that the combination of experienced observers, fixed census routes and open terrain has provided population estimates with little or no bias”. Even less documentary material is available to determine the quality of surveys the further one reaches into the past. 

As the first post-World War II director Renzo Videsott reported, during the interwar years, instead of counting ibex following set routes, wardens would first attract animals to specific spots with salt and then count how many came there over three days in a row. Geographer Giuseppe Barbieri remarked in 1947 that it was easy to imagine the lack of care with which the delicate counting operations were performed by the members of the Milizia, who were often sent to the Gran Paradiso as a form of punishment for discipline breaches. As he noted, “the least that could happen was that a group of ibex or chamois, moving, would be counted twice and another would not be counted at all.” 

A further question is whether the censuses reflected real numbers or the varying desire of different actors at different times to frame the Milizia’s conservation efforts, in either a good or a bad light, for political and propaganda reasons.
However, the fact that the same numbers appear in both a post-World War II record—when the park’s new administrators were striving to outline the fallacies, faults, and shortcomings of the Fascist Milizia—and in a report written in the 1940s by one of the officers of just the corps that managed the park after 1933 seems to support the consistency of the data. Even should we suspect that the census numbers may be biased for some other reason, the severity of the post-1933 population decrease appears to be confirmed since this is supported by documents produced under radically different data production regimes.

On the other hand, one could think that the pre-1933 data, which had already been published in a 1935 article written by another Milizia officer, are an exaggerated representation of the real trend, produced for reasons of Fascist
propaganda. However, the fact that the same data were also used in the post-World War II account, and that Italian conservationists always praised the park’s autonomous administration’s good conservation practices prior to 1933, may imply that, again, they are at least accurate in giving an idea of the trend.

Another bias that could affect data gathered in the Fascist era censuses is linked to the relationship between the number of wardens and the number of ibex counted. It is possible that the reduced number of wardens after the Milizia took over full responsibility for the park did not actually lead to the presumed worsening of surveillance, subsequent increase in poaching rates, and, thus, an actual reduction in stock. It might instead have caused a less effective census methodology or a lack of data for some areas, leading to fewer animals being counted (or estimated) than those that were actually there. Without any independent sources to consult, it is almost impossible to determine which way the causal relationship went.

As regards the matter of the new wardens’ lack of expertise and local know-how as compared to those hired by the park administration before 1933, I lean towards an actual reduction of stock rather than a mere underestimation. Milizia wardens were, in fact, still accompanied by expert local auxiliary wardens with direct knowledge of the territory until, at least, 1941. Hence, I feel confident in asserting that the interwar animal censuses accurately represent the ibex population trend (if not their exact numbers). The historical research work done here to ensure the viability of the data is nonetheless important as it may contribute to explain the mechanics of surveying more and how the affected the conservation policies.

Counting animals serves, at least, two different needs. On the one side, as said earlier, it is connected to the global attempts at saving populations and species. On the other, in a local dimension, it has often been used to justify hunting and culling: the geophysical limitations imposed by conservation itself, that is by the drawing of a border to create a reservation in which animals should be preserved created in fact artificially ecological stresses.

For instance, in the process that led to the institution of conservation in Tanzania, through the historical imprint of German colonization and British mandate administration, the latter at first imposed a system in which animal control had the priority over conservation. Baseline conditions produced by animal counts and reconstructions, or to be precise, their representation affects in fact the direction of conservation policies: if the ideal past is thought to have smaller numbers culling will take precedence over preservation and vice versa.

Counting from the ground in the open savanna of the Serengeti is a taxing and almos impossible work. Proxies have thus often been used to measure the size and impact of animal populations: in Tanzania British consultants used trampled riversides and lakesides to assess the population numbers were too high for the size of the Serengeti national park, leading them to advise for culling. The idea of "carrying capacity", discussed already earlier, was central to many mid-century park creation efforts. When the animal population remained at or below the ideal numerical capacity, the inhabitants had room to grow, and the system could eventually reach a point of oscillating equilibrium between predators and prey, grazers and grasses, soil and vegetation. Once the animal numbers exceeded that capacity, however, overconsumption of the grasses would tip the system out of balance, leading to an accellerating ecological decline and widespread animal starvation. This lead to a widespread fear of competition for resources by livestock. A fear common to many parks around the world, including the Gran Paradiso discussed earlier.

Plans to enclose the Ngorongoro crater area nearby Serengeti National Park in the late 1950s were the reason German conservationists Bernhard and Michael Grzimek begun the first aerial surveys of animal migrations between the two areas. Thanks to their new counting methods they were able to show how the migrations followed different patterns than expected.

Through their trial-and-error efforts to count, track, and analyze the foraging habits of migrating animals ... the Grzimeks hoped to follow the 1933 London Convention mandates by demarcating the entire habitat of the dwindling populations of wandering wildebeest and zebra. Unlike previous surveys the Grzimek made use of the whole arsenal of scientific wildlife management to establish a firm baseline for future conservation efforts. (SLIDE GRZIMEK) Fully automatic camera's developed for military surveys during WWII were used as animal surveying instruments. The airplane itself they were using was the first kind of airplane allowed by the Allies to be built in Germany after a 10 year ban following the war. The creation of a bird's eye view of the land de-territorialised it, cancelling boundaries and human land uses in a process that can be termed the rhetorical rewilding of a historical landscape. As in North America, native populations were framed as disturbances to the natural state of the area, whose current and historical imprint needed to be erased.

A series of automated photographs, each calibrated to overlap by 50% with previous and following pictures created a strip of film that, according to the Grzimeks would allow to improve the precision of numerical estimates made only from the ground. A method that failed, at least for the migrating wildebeest and zebra. The lack of geographical markers on the grond made the effort to recombine the map, as developed in topographical photogrammetry after WWI, made it cost prohibitive. The herds ended thus to be counted from the air by the passenger, a method prone to the risk of over and undercounting.

The photographic efforts allowed the Grzimeks however to prove that Lake Natron, NE of Ngorongoro, only hosted 164.000 flamingoes instead of the million birds ornithologists were speaking of at the time on the basis of traditional methods. 

The airplane promised viewers an overarching, "unbiased" perspective of the plains unavailable to anybody on the ground. Nonetheless, both the survey made by the Grzimek's and the previous, more traditional one executed by British ecologist Pearsall a few years before created a baseline that dehistoricised the landscape, cancelling multiple layers of human/animal interactions and the footprint of colonialism and idealised an ahistorical migration pattern: this was a "critical step to the Serengeti 'becoming wilderness' in the 1950s." (TL 110)

Environmental historians have, in the last 40 years, extensively analyzed how the concepts of landscape, wilderness, nature, and environment (all essential to certain forms of romantic and post-romantic tourism) have been rhetorically construed and depend on historical and cultural contingencies. Animal populations and their counting are similarly social constructs, created for the specific needs of conservation and hunting management. Building on the invented tradition concept elsewhere I have talked of _invented nature_. I dare to say that, all things considered, we could talk also of _invented populations_.
