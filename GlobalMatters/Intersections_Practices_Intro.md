### Session II: Practices

**Conveners: _Alejandra Osorio & Wilko Hardenberg_**

**Questions**

Can attention to the history of practices help to overcome the micro/macro and local/global divides?

- Local practices become global and vice versa. In both instances there is the need to adapt them to different conditions. How does this tension affect our understanding of when a certain practice becomes something completely different?
- In which way can a focus on practices help to integrate pre-industrial and non-Western knowledge and technological traditions into the global history of technology?
- Switching to an environmental perspective, how do local perceptions of variation relate to global understandings of change? What are the epistemic tools we use to make sense of change?

**Introduction**

In a recent paper Angelika Epple makes a call for a practice turn in global history.[^epple] In this session we want to discuss if, and in case how, such a turn may contribute to overcome the micro/macro and local/global binaries in the histories of science, technology, and the environment. Moreover we want do look at whether, and in which terms. overcoming such divides is actually a desirable outcome and in which terms "global microhistories" are a viable path.[^micro]

Practice theory is a widely recognised scoiological approach, based on the works of Bourdieu and Foucault. To adopt it as is may, we fear, limit the effectiveness of looking at practices as a way to to understand the histories of science, technology and the environment and/or force the discussion into a too narrow direction. We suggest thus here a very broad, less theoretical interpretation of "practices" as a basis for our discussion: the way we do things and how it affects our relationship with a variety of social groupings (families, societies, cultures, states). The idea here is to offer neither a bottom up nor a top down perspective, but, in line with the idea to possibly overcome the micro/macro divide, look also at ways in which the two productively interact.

Epple suggests that the main difficulty in doing global history is to combine the need to preserve the poliphony of individual actions that make up history, while providing some coherence to the narrative arc. Existing global history, she remarks, haven't always been effective in reaching both of these aims. An attention for practices, she suggests, may contribute to shift the attention of global history from the global as a spatial unit to the question of how global entanglements can be productively understood.

One example of how practices may prove useful in this undertaking is to be found in the stresses of fieldwork. An attention to how things were done and how this doing was the product of unequal social interactions may in fact help to overcome the issue of trying make sense of how cosmopolitan/global knowledge is produced from an accumulation of very local, very specific forms of knowledge.[^networks] It may also contribute informing our understanding of how the knowledge "extracted" from a specific locale in this knowledge globalization process is the uncredited product of traditional forms of knowledge.[^elnino]
