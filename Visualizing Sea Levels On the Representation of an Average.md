**Title**: Visualizing Sea Levels: On the Representation of an Average

**Author**: Wilko Graf von Hardenberg, Ph.D., Max Planck Institute for the History of Science. Boltzmannstr. 22, 14195 Berlin, whardenberg@mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de

I am a senior research scholar at the Max Planck Institute for the History of Science in Berlin, where I coordinate the *Art of Judgement* research cluster. Trained as a political historian and a geographer in Turin, Brussels, and Cambridge, my research activities focus on the intersection between the history of science and technology and environmental history, paying particular attention to issues relating to environmental change and its measurement, socio-ecological conflicts, and nature conservation. I recently held the post of guest professor in the *Diversity of Knowledge* interdisciplinary study programme of Humboldt University in Berlin, where I taught interpretations, perceptions, and politics of the oceans in the modern age. Prior to moving to Berlin I worked at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, the Rachel Carson Center for Environment and Society and the Deutsches Museum in Munich, the University of Trento, and the Scuola Normale Superiore in Pisa. 

**Abstract**: Mean sea level is the most widely adopted metric to assess relative changes in the positioning of land and sea along the world’s littorals. It also has long been the most used reference point for altitude measurements. In reason of its diffusion it has by now become naturalised. That is, we mention it without really thinking about what it means. We tend thus to forget that­—rather than a natural marker of anything—it is a product of technically and culturally determined assumptions.

In truth mean sea level is an extremely abstract way to conceptualize and rationalize the boundary between land and sea as one, computable but not actually tangible, point in space. As such, it is clearly an artefact of the cartographic need to provide stable, long-term depictions of spaces that are actually in constant reciprocal movement. In comparison to other levels of the sea, such as highest high or lowest low tides, it lacks their physical and visual perceivability and can only be determined through technical means. 

In this paper I will give an account of how the visualization of sea levels developed in about two centuries since before the first self-registering tide gauges until the deployment of geodetic satellites. After providing some early examples of representations of tidal extremes and their role in cartography I will lead the audience through how the mechanization and mathematization of measurements transformed sea levels into curves. Finally I will look at how the increase in available data series led over time to the extrapolation of sea level rise trends, to then conclude with how sea levels are represented by contemporary remote sensing and mapping technologies. In the process I expect to clarify how our historically determined ideas of sea level and its visualizations influence our long term understanding of global environmental change.

**Keywords**: Sea level, measurement, visualization
