## The end of an endemy. Social and environmental conditions of the decline of malaria in the ricescapes of northwestern Italy

The waterlogged rice fields of the western Po plain in northern Italy have, as many other rice cultivation regions throughout the world, been plagued for decades by malarial outbreaks. The disease was once endemic in the region, reaching, in the first half of the 1880s almost pandemic levels in some areas. In the middle of a political struggle around agrarian regulations aimed at reducing the risk of malarial epidemics, and contrary to the coeval experience in other regions of the world, in the twentieth century malaria became, however, less and less common in the Italian rice cultivation region. By the early 1930s medical doctors and agronomists declared that the disease had been all but defeated in the area. 

Coeval explanations of this unexpected, epochal transformation ranged around two main poles: the role of social and economic enhancements on the one hand and that of ecological modifications on the other. The first grouping included improvements in hygienic conditions, higher quality of life, free distribution of quinine, and a variety of agronomical enhancements. The other focused on physical modifications leading to increased resistance or immunity to the parasites causing malarial infections in either the mosquitoes or humans.

In this paper I will focus on this globally unique development and delve into how an area subjected to increasingly intense irrigation conditions turned from frequent pandemic outbursts to almost complete recovery in less than fifty years. In doing this I will provide insights, through the detailed analysis of a case study, in the complex interrelationship between landscapes, agricultural practices, vectors, and human societies.
