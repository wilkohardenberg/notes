Charles Lallemand (1857-1938) was a French geodesist, a member of the Bureau des Longitudes, and the executive secretary of the Service du Nivellement, the French ordnance survey. It was in this latter capacity that he worked on developing and perfectioning various technical instruments that proved essential for geodetical work. In this paper I will focus on the "médimarémetre," a mean sea level measuring instrument intended to simplify the determination of the vertical datum. Building on sources from the Bureau and beyond, I will briefly address the rise and fall this instrument. Beside searching the BdL database for medimarimeter, I did the same with the minutes of the International Association of Geodesy, which have been digitised and made available online as part of a project I launched at the MPIWG (SLIDE 2).

But let's first give some context: The “constant agitation” of the oceans, as it was termed by German geodesist Friedrich Robert Helmert, has always made it difficult to determine the actual mean sea level, that is a continuous average of all the heights reached by the water over multiple days. Attempts to overcome this had been made already by Robert Moray in the seventeenth century, with the proposal to use stilling-wells to reduce the impact of waves and winds on the exactness of measurements. The introduction in the 1830s of automated self-registering tide gauges, allowing to trace the curve of the tide on paper for later analysis, contributed significantly to revising the way in which the level of the sea was conceived. The production of a more or less continuous flow of data about the relative height of land and sea at some select locations allowed its average to be actually computed, rather than just assumed. The idea of a mean sea level was conceived at precisely that time, building upon William Whewell’s intuition, published in 1837, that a mean tide level would provide a better approximation of an oceanic equipotential surface than either a high or low tide marker, and would thus represent a better reference plane for vertical measurements. During the second half of the nineteenth century, mean sea level was adopted, also thanks to the diplomatic efforts of Charles Lallemand, as the standard datum by most European countries.[^lallemand]

Self-registering tide gauges or mareographs, like the one Lallemand had installed in Marseille in 1884 (SLIDE 3), were however complex and expensive to set up and maintain, making it inconvenient to install multiple ones in different ports. Because of this in the 1890s at least a third of the measurement stations along Europe’s coasts still used simple scaled gauges, rather than the more modern automated tide-gauges. To achieve a comparable level of precision to self-registering gauges the effective measurement of the level of the sea on an analogue gauge would require at least two officers who, with the help of a chronometer, would regularly register the height of the tide. This ought to be done, according to recommendations, every 5 minutes for a substantial amount of time before and after both high and low tide. It was definitely an endeavour requiring great discipline and manpower, especially considering that the same process was done simultaneously in multiple locations to register differences between local measurements.  In an 1829  account of the hydrographic works along France’s western coasts, the hydrographer-in-chief Charles François Beautemps-Beaupré remarked how hard it was to find men devoted enough to the needs of the hydrographic service to accept to spend six months alone in the same small village along the coast to observe every change in the level of the sea with impeccable regularity. Even harder, he quipped, it appeared to be to convince them not to mark anything in their logs whenever they forgot to take a measurement or were not able to. And even in the unlikely case that men that could be completely trusted were found, there was a whole range of possible causes of error: among others the displacement of a gauge, the failure of a clock, or just mere bad weather.[^beautemps]

The medimarimeter, (SLIDE 4) first described publicly in 1887 in an Annex to the minutes of the IAG, is a much simpler and cheaper instrument than an automated tide gauge, allowing for a rapid multiplication of the points of measurement in France and abroad. Furthermore, automatically giving a reading for mean sea level, it made the work of the officers charged with the task much easier. Lallemand's main aim in developing the medimarimeter was, in fact, to create a tool that would make it less costly to measure sea levels and make it thus possible to increase the number of locations where it was measured.@lallemand_medimaremetre 

The principle behind the medimarimeter is, essentially, the same of a stilling well: passing through a capillary canal or a porous surface a wave will reduce its amplitude and delay its phase, without the mean level being affected. The daily tide is being thus reduced to an almost insignifcant oscillation. The reduced amplitude allows for simplified calculations. Furthermore, one observation per day is sufficient to register the variation of level over time. To measure the level a chemically treated strip of paper is immersed in the water within the well (SLIDE 5). The paper becomes almost immediately black, allowing to read the level of the sea and compare it with previous measurements.

After a succesful testing phase in Marseille, where it was run against the more costly local mareograph to check the instrument's reliability, multiple medimarimeters—costing, it has to be considered, less than a hundredth of a self-registering tide gauge (200-300 franc vs 40.000)—were installed at 14 locations in France alone, one more in Belgium, and six in Italy.

Lallemand was obviously, and rightfully, proud of his instrument. A quick search of the Bureau des Longitudes and International Association of Geodesy database brings up nonetheless some criticism. 

Immediately, in response to the paper in which he first presented the instrument, the Italian representative at the International Association of Geodesy, Alessandro Betocchi stated that, while he found Lallemand's solution very ingenious, he doubted the quality of the results. He wonders how often the poreous surface has to be changed to avoid incrustation that may skew the measurements: in fact, he claims, at every change the measurement baseline will be affected. Lallemand replied that the tube in Marseille has been left unchanged for 2 years and a half without affecting the quality of measurements.(IE 1887)

Adolph Hirsch, the association's secretary, was instead disturbed by the fact that Lallemand had written that mean sea level as measured is only a temporary vertical datum, as the level of the ocean is subject to long term variations due to the planet's cooling and the impact of meteors. Hirsch wonders why Lallemand would have gone through all the effort to develop a new mean sea level measurement tool if he doesn't believe that a permanent mean sea level actually exists. In his reply Lallemand doesn't really address this point, but highlights how recorded crustal movements impact continuously the relative position of land and sea. Hirsch had the last word, and remarked how just the fact that the land masses were moving made it necessary to believe in an immutable sea level.

Hyppolite Fizeau was one of the most critical voices within the Bureau. Already in 1888 he noted how the dialysis process may affect the registered level in case the density of water weren't the same on both sides of the filter and that water density on the surface may be different than at depth because of the influence of fresh water coming from rivers (6 June 1888). Also the renown geodesist Hervé Faye had some doubts about the usefulness of Lallemand's instrument, as, in opposition to traditional mareographes it would not register the impact of meteorological or exceptional geological events (such as the Krakatoa eruption of 1883). This however, it must be noted, was just the aim of Lallemand in developing this tool: producing a simple instrument that would allow to register geodetical relevant sea level readings, without the hassle of having to consider extraneous forces (3 October 1888).

The debate went on in bits and pieces in the following years. In 1890 Fizeau remarked how the medimarimeter wasn't deemed trustworthy by physicists (15 June 1890). The filtering surface is an obstacle, whose effectiveness may well be significantly affected by the actual dirtiness and viscosity of sea water. Fizeau is worried that the medimarimeter may become the only instrument used to measure sea levels, which would make unavailable a mass of data to researchers interested in the impact of meteorological influences. The engineer of the hydrographic services, soon to become its director, Anatole Bouque de la Grye reassured Fizeau that the medimarimeter won't be the only tool employed to measure sea level. Everybody is aware of the fact that, as it delays registering of any change by 15 hours and hugely reduces their amplitudes, the instrument is unsuitable to measure the role of meteorologically induced waves (9 July 1890).

Nonetheless (SLIDE 6), despite all criticism, the little cost of the instrument and Lallemand's role within the Service de Nivellement granted the medimarimeter a firm place among the tools used by the Service du Nivellement. New medimarimeters were regularly installed, including two on the southern shores of the Mediterranean,  in Tunisia and Algeria. A further development of the instrument, a self-registering medimarimeter was then installed in Boulogne (6 Jan. 1892).

The wide network of medimarimeters was even proposed, later in 1892, as a way to define a new international standard vertical datum as part of the ongoing debate of the International Association of Geodesy at this regard (7 Dec. 1892). This never happened, but for over a decade no further comments appear to have been made within the Bureau about the medimarimeters, which probably ended up being accepted as a standard tool for a specific task. At the international level the minutes of the geodetical association register, until well into the twentieth century, the continued installation of medimarimeters in various parts of the world, such as Chile and Ecuador (SLIDE 7). Other sources discuss new installations well into the 1920s .@toker2020

In 1905 however Lallemand himself intervened to note that a systematic difference of abou 2 cm had been registered over the years between the data given by the mareographs and those registered with the medimarimeters. 

On January 31, 1910 Lallemand  presented at the Academie des Sciences a paper at this regard. Due to the removal of a few drops  of water each time the level is measured, readings have regularly been too low. Even if only a few millimteres are actually removed, the delay produced by the porous surface doesn't allow the medimarimeter to be refilled completely by the following measurement. The difference between the tube and the exterior increases thus daily.
