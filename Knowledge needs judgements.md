**Knowledge production needs judgements**. 

These come, obviously, in different shapes: bias, assessments, estimates, value judgements, to name only a few. 

- But how exactly are different forms of judgement construed? 
- How do politics affect their development? 
- What about the social and environmental conditions within which they are made? 
- What does it take it to gather the information necessary to make judgements? 
- Finally, how do judgements inform the processes by which decisions are taken? 

These are some of the assumptions and questions that inform the research agenda of the _Art of Judgement_ cluster. 

Within this broad framework, over the last three years, the cluster has increasingly focused its attention on the twin issues of **how radical changes have been apprehended by science** and of **how science and technology developed in response to major social and environmental changes**. The "art of judgement" is clearly central in both these issues, as no change can be registered if there is no method to assess and judge it.

What interests us is thus the in-depth analysis of the historical dimension and context in which judgements about change have been produced, paying attention in particular to the relationship between power structures and knowledge.

A core issue of the cluster's research agenda has become, over time, the historicization of reference points (such as the sea level) and baselines (for example in nature conservation) that have nowadays become integral elements in our discourses about anthropogenic environmental change. 

The study of these elements attempts to provide answers to the pivotal question of how environmental conditions existing at a given time in specific locales frame the development of scientific disciplines. 

It contributes thus to explore how the environment itself affects the ways in which it is understood and interpreted and, more broadly, how environmental historical approaches may provide innovative ways to approace the history of science and technology.
