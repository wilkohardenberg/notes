---
title: Historicity of data
date: 2021-10-26
author: Wilko Hardenberg
---

The image we have of our current climate crisis is the product of complex digitally informed modelling efforts.  

Such efforts run on data: there is no other way to ‘see’ trends on a global scale than through the modeling of huge amounts of both contemporary and historical data. 

Not only scaling up or change of focus (local ---> global, past ---> present, immediate ---> mediate, biased ---> neutral). All of these dimensions coexist in any given moment.

To register rates of change and order the world these data need to be assembled into baselines through social and scientific processes.

These digitally informed analysis methods (models, data, baselines) build our world: environing technologies.

Full understanding of how they impact our comprehension of the world asks to unpack them into their many political, cultural, and material components. 

Last 40 years characterized by the institution of whole new networks of institutions and infrastructures aimed at producing and sharing controlled data, including repositories of historical data. 

To improve our scientific understanding of the ongoing global changes more and more data are necessary, from both the present and the past, to feed our models, construe our baselines, and develop our policies.

The reconstruction of data series beyond late modernity and beyond the North Atlantic is crucial to help refining and improving modeling and thus understanding how our world changes over time.

But historical data produce new challenges: produced in different times with different technologies and different aims, they are the outcome of numerous human, technical, and environmental variables. 

A historical outlook is intrinsic to much research done to understand and counteract the epochal environmental changes to which humanity is subjecting the planet. Historical, not born digital data represent, in fact, unrepeatable measurements of the state of the environment.

Even if there seems to be a huge overlap in needs and intentions in face of these new needs for historical data, the interaction between the professional historians and archivists and the scientists using these data has not always proven to be easy 

The historians’ core skill, that is the ability to contextualize and explain how data were recorded and transmitted in the past has not always been considered central by the scientific community. 

But the ability to counteract and revert the processes of “datafication" that have made events and objects into data (through the loss of metadata) may prove crucial in offering a critical outlook on the processes and socio-political backgrounds by which data are produced. 

And this is not only an issue that regards descriptive and qualitative sources. 

Because of their clearly quantitative nature modern instrumental recordings may be seen as exemplary data, they do so because the way they have been collected was designed for a precise purpose, allowing them to achieve completeness in representing just a sliver of reality. 

Numerous contextual variables will however be lost in the refinement process that led from event to data. A practice so common in the sciences that only little documentation is usually provided at its regard. Once “scrubbed” and “cleaned” data become de-historicized, cancelling the complex network of relationships that contributed to form them.

TO CONCLUDE: Climate modeling, the production of an intelligible image of how humanity is modifying the world is a collegial effort that requires a combination of digital and analog methods. Present and historical data are social products, that need to be maintained. Knowledge about how data have been and are produced is crucial in view of a better understanding of climate change and its consequences.
