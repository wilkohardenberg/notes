#### Presentation of *Sea Level: A History* 

_Wilko Graf von Hardenberg_

I propose to present my book *Sea Level: A History* in dialogue with Giacomo Parrinello (Sciences Po, Paris), whose work on the history of water sciences and the environmental history of coasts offers a complementary perspective to the story I tell about sea level. In my book, in fact, I trace how sea level has emerged over the last couple of centuries as both a scientific reference point and a powerful cultural framework that continues to influence how we conceptualize and respond to environmental change. Jointly we aim to highlight how the history of scientific measurement and baselines is relevant to the long term environmental history of the liminal spaces where land and sea meet.

As the primary metric used to assess relative changes in the positioning of land and sea along the world's coasts the level of the sea has, in recent years, become a trope of climate discourse. It also has long been the most used reference point for altitude measurements. It has become so common that the fact that it is actually a construct is often forgotten.

The story I tell in my book begins in the early 18th century when the first altitude measurements referenced to sea level appeared on maps, marking a shift from relative height measurements to standardized systems. Through examining the development of measurement technologies—from early tide gauges to self-recording devices and eventually satellites—I reveal how the material properties of water intersected with cultural desires for precision and standardization. The development of "mean sea level" as a concept reflects not just technological advancement, but also changing philosophical understandings of nature and measurement.

The use of sea level as both a metric for change and a baseline for altitudes is tightly connected with a long perceived Holocenic stability and its recent upheaval. In dialogue with Dr. Parrinello I will offer some insights on how mean sea level was first defined, to then become the prime geodetic reference point and, finally, a powerful metric of the Anthropocene. The fact that the sea is rising due to human influence is fairly recent. The awareness of this fact even more so. But the means we use to assess and measure it are rooted in history, originated within cultural, social, and scientific agendas that were radically different from current ones.

National reference points for altitude, for instance, emerged through complex political negotiations, while the global network of measurement stations followed the pathways of European colonial empires. These political dimensions continue to influence how we measure and conceptualize sea level today and thus also our understanding of the interaction between land and water.

This historical analysis of sea level demonstrates how water's material properties and our methods of measuring and conceptualizing it have profound implications for environmental knowledge, political decisions, and cultural understanding. As we confront accelerating climate change, understanding the constructed nature of sea level as a baseline can contribute to more effective and nuanced responses to global environmental challenges.

**Wilko Graf von Hardenberg** is a Berlin-based historian of science and the environment. He holds an Italian laurea in history and a PhD in geography from the University of Cambridge. At the core of his work lies the issue of how nature has been made, in the modern age, into a measurable entity, how this has affected environmental policy-making, and how the state has become a pivotal actor in the maintenance of the natural world. He has been a visiting assistant professor at the University of Wisconsin–Madison and a senior research scholar at the Max Planck Institute for the History of Science in Berlin. His latest achievement is the publication of *Sea Level: A History* with University of Chicago Press (2024). Preliminary work on this topic appeared in the *Journal of Historical Geography* and *Isis*. Prior to that he published the first history of Italy's Gran Paradiso National Park, *A Monastery for the Ibex* (University of Pittsburgh Press, 2021), co-authored *Mussolini's Nature* (MIT Press, 2021), and co-edited *The Nature State* (Routledge, 2017). He is now the co-PI of the AHRC/DFG funded international research project "The Sound of Nature: Soundscapes and Environmental Awareness, 1750-1950" at Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin.

**Giacomo Parrinello** is an associate professor of environmental history at the Centre for History at the Paris Institute of Political Studies. He holds a PhD in history, geography, and law from the University of Siena. He has been a Carson Fellow at the Rachel Carson Center in Munich, as well as a Marie Curie Fellow at Louisiana State University and the Institute of Social Ecology in Vienna. He works on a broad range of topics, focusing in recent years particularly on water-related themes. He is the author of *Fault Lines* (Berghahn, 2015), an environmental history of earthquakes, their consequences, and urbanism in 20th-century Southern Italy, which has received multiple awards. He is currently working on a book about the extraordinary intensification of water use and control in the Po Valley of Italy from the late eighteenth to the early twenty-first century. Early findings from this project have appeared in *Environment and History*, *Technology and Culture*, and *Water History*. He is also interested in the history of water sciences, the environmental history of Mediterranean European coasts, the history of river sediment, and the interdisciplinary study of rivers and society.
