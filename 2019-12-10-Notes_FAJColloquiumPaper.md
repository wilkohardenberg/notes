Finn Arne's paper leads us away from the beaten track, towards the world of cabin, with the ever unfilled promise to provide us with the most real experience of nature. In doing so it tackles the role of cabins in respect to Norwegian national identity, the romanticization of the 'old life'—a classic feature of all 'back to nature' movements, the fact that a connection with nature is, tipically, the 'other side of the medal': it only becomes possible/desirable/evident with industrialization.

Finn Arne shows in broad strokes how, as most of such socio-cultural trends, from backpacking to conservationism, the spread of cabins lives in an intrinsic irony: they, quite heavily, touch nature while allowing their owners to have the impression of experiencing 'untouched nature'. It fits squarely in what I've called elsewhere, praphrasing Hobsbawm and Ranger, 'invented natures'. These, I wrote: 

> can be defined as those environments to which human society attributes a particular degree of naturalness, or which are hailed as exceptional in comparison to highly anthropic regions (such as urban settings, industrial regions, or farmland), but are actually still a product of the historical interaction between human societies and the non-human environment.

In this respect I wonder about what is truly exceptional in the Norwegian experience. The cabins are a great example, but not an unicum: other kinds of infrastructures, from hiking trails to high mountain roads, from national parks to skiing resorts, to name just a few example I'm familiar with, purpose to give people the ability to 'experience nature'. Wilderness, to quote Cronon, rather than a transcendent nature is 'a product of that civilization, and could hardly be contaminated by the very stuff of which it is made'. I would appreciate, in a full paper, a tighter connection to this literature and a clearer take on what makes this case stand apart.

It would be nice to stress further how Ålhytta can be seen as exemplary of the broader trend, while it is such a niche product. I get its recognition as cultural heritage in and by itself, but I remain wondering how much it can tell to the international audience you aim to reach about the broader issues related to Norwegian cabins. I, for once, remain curious about the environmental impact of the remaining 99.5% of the market and of how such major transformation of nature can be marketed as 'getting in touch with nature'. I realise that I might just want to read your book, if it just weren't in Norwegian.

The main thing that I feel is still missing, especially in view of a submission to _Environmental History_, is, beyond the section titles, a more in-depth take the place of the huts in the environment: if cabin life has become an extension of city life it must have cause a major transformation of landscapes. Could you tell us abit more on how, in public discourse, these new landscapes are still perceived as 'untouched nature'.

Among the very minor things, it would be nice to know what 'Stev om stav og stolpe' means. Google translate gave me something like log on log on log, but it would be nice to have a better grasp of it. And also, around that point in the narrative, I was unclear about which model actually became the Ålhytta.

Furthermore, could you tell us the connection between the company and the municipality? The contest was sponsored, you say, by the municipality, but I remained unclear about whether the company also was a product of this municipal effort. I wondered if the strong normative character of the company might have been connected to its (possible) origins as a public venture for the common good.

To conclude, I believe this paper provides a fascinating insight in a crucial case of the manufacturing of the nature experience, which, while there are many antecedents that could be and have been analysed, has clearly played such an important role in the post-WWII world. I _just_ would want from you to provide a better grasp of the larger system you hint to and to the specific aspects of the Norwegian 'ideas of authenticity'.
