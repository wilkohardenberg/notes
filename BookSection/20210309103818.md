The Art of Judgement research cluster looks at the ways in which human assessments of environmental change, a very specific, and increasingly important, set of judgements, are formed. Set at the crossroads of environmental history and history of science the group is born out of an urgent desire to clarify both the ways in which societies historically faced changes in the natural world and explore the conceptual and material tools use to measure them. In doing so the cluster aims at producing new perspectives on how “change” can be framed interdisciplinarily. In recent years the cluster has focused on exploring judgements of change in two fields in particular: the earth sciences and nature conservation. While apparently distant the two fields are connected by a common dependency on baselines as means to gauge change over time. And just through a dedicated working group the cluster has looked into the social, cultural and institutional contexts in which environmental baselines emerge as objects of scientific analysis and public policy. According to our inquiry baselines used as benchmarks for quantitative modelling and forecasting are not fixed indices of a more ecologically robust past. Instead, they are shifting artifacts of cultural imagination, debates over historical memory, the nexus of power and knowledge, and the distributed agency of human, non-human, and post-human actors operating at multiple geographic and temporal scales. From the methodological point of view the cluster is particularly interested in exploring the means by which the digital humanities can contribute to frame our understanding of the historical development and apprehension of environmental change. At this regard we have begun working in particular on a corpus of conference minutes produced in the late nineteenth century by what later has become the International Association of Geodesy, digitized in cooperation with the GeoForschungsZentrum in Potsdam. Our work finds expression in a lively discussion group that reaches well beyond institutional boundaries.
