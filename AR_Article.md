#### Gauging the sea

Tide gauges play a crucial role in our ability to assess variations in the global level of the sea. Across the centuries they have experienced a number of crucial technological developments. The first ones were, to put it bluntly, just sticks in the mud against which the constant movements of the sea could be checked. In 1665 then, the natural philosopher Robert Moray suggested to use a stilling well  and floating device to reduce the impact of waves on measurements. This basic infrastructure was further improved in 1831, when the first self-registering tide gauge was installed at the Royal Docks in Sheerness, on the mouth of the River Medway, in close proximity of the mouth of the River Thames. The addition of a clockwork mechanism and a pen made it possible to automatically draw all the levels reached by the sea over time on a drum of paper, essentially without human intervention. 

Apparently trivial, this was a significant leap forward when it came to the reframing of sea level as a globally accepted reference point. The mechanisation of the measurement process revolutionised, in fact, how mean sea level was calculated, transforming it from an average of extremes, as had been the case for earlier calculations of mean tide level, into a continuous process. Yet adoption was gradual. While organizations like the British Admiralty quickly installed automated gauges at key ports, such as Plymouth and Portsmouth, the majority of coastal stations had no clear use case for continuous, automated recording. By the end of the century, roughly a third of Europe's tide gauges remained manual and part of those did not even include a stilling well.

Traditional gauges, however, had a massive limitation, as they required a significant amount of work and dedication. A couple of years before the first automated tide-gauge was installed, the leading French cartographer Charles-François Beautemps-Beaupré lamented how hard it was to find reliable observers, who could be trusted to collect data on tidal variations for long stretches of time in isolated coastal villages. The extremely detailed instructions about how often tide poles had to be checked produced by the British Astronomer Royal George Biddell Airy as part of his work on the 1842 survey of Ireland, give an idea of how demanding the task was: to obtain reliable data it was necessary to perform seventy observations a day. 

Overcoming part of these issues and facilitating the collection of more data points beyond high and low water, automation played a central role in making mean sea level into a widely accepted vertical datum, that is a standard reference point for the measurement of elevations on land. This was possible primarily by providing the technical means to produce reliable and reproduceable calculations of mean sea level. As a consequence, mean sea level spread globally as the hegemonic reference point for elevations on land. This did not mean, however, that all nations approached the selection of their vertical datum in the same way.

In Great Britan, for instance, the institutional vertical datum varied multiple times to accommodate technical and theoretical advancements. The datum was initially set arbitrarily, serving only as a reference point from which to measure, albeit in a brief manual campaign, mean sea level at Victoria Dock in Liverpool, which was then adopted in 1844 as the new nation-wide datum. Over the following decades the Ordnance Survey recorded increasing discrepancies between the datum and field measurements at various locations. By the turn of the century this led to the decision to determine a new national datum. According to plan this should have been an average of three different stations spread uniformly along the coasts of Great Britain. The idea behind this choice was to produce a datum that would be representative of the variations registered in mean sea level along the whole coast of Britain. The plan crumbled, however, due to its complexity and in the end the measurements taken between 1915 and 1921 at just one of the three planned stations, in Newlyn, Cornwall, were selected as the new national datum. The grandly named Newlyn Tidal Observatory is hidden in an unassuming building at the very end of a wharf and hides a tidal gauge that is technically almost identical to the first one set up at Sheerness almost a century earlier.

This British story of datums moving along the coasts reveals an underlying tension between the idea that mean sea level is in some way a 'natural' reference point and the Ordnance Survey's need for long-term stability. Changing the national datum according to technological advances was costly,  requiring new maps and risking confusion rather than clarity. Consequently, the realization that datums are conventions rather than representations of some physical phenomenon slowly took hold.

A place where this idea was explicit was the recently unified German Empire in the 1870s. Each of the different states that composed it had already developed their own reference framework, usually referring to the closest sea. Once the country unified, the potential chaos caused by a  multiplicity of  datums, stirred up a debate among geodesists about the need to define a so-called _Reichshorizont_, an "imperial horizon." A dedicated commission was set up, that expressed serious doubts about the viability of a measured mean sea level on the Baltic as the datum. Ongoing measurements suggested that the sea was at slant going from east to west, making it difficult to favour one station over another. Moreover, committee members expressed doubts that it was even possible to measure mean sea level reliably from the coast. The committee's final suggestion was thus to effectively decouple the datum from gauge measurements. To do so a permanent marker ought to be installed at a geologically stable inland location, which, somewhat conveniently, seemed to be the Reich's capital, Berlin. In 1879 the height of the new national datum, the _Normalnull_, triangulated throughout northern Germany from the level of high water at the port of Amsterdam (a reference point long used across the region) was engraved  on a wall of the Berlin Observatory. The physical location of the marker was moved a few times over the years, but the _Normalnull_ remained the official German datum until 1992.

The technological development of sea level measurements has followed a punctuated pattern: not only radical improvements have been rare, as suggested by the self-registering tide gauge's origin story, they were mostly based on gradual build-ups and marked by a significant lag in the spatial diffusion of new methods. For instance, by 1980 the slow decay and frequent breakdowns of the existing, Nazi-installed, network of mechanical float gauges had convinced the Norwegian authorities to explore the possibility of a complete overhaul. The Norwegian Geographical Survey drew up a detailed wishlist, that included abandoning stilling wells, digital timekeeping and data recording, and automated data transmission. Not all of these could be implemented. After attempts with acoustic gauges, which proved still to be an immature technology, it became clear that stilling wells, the oldest technological element of any tide gauge, were essentially unreplaceable. If structurally and physically the new gauges did not look that different from their predecessors, the processes within them were set up for a new digital era, including digital-first data over the whole network and the possibility to have real-time centralised analysis, significantly improving nation-wide environmental monitoring.

Meanwhile, the work of assessing global mean sea level has moved to a broad extent the farthest away possible from coasts, to orbit, from where satellites measure variations in the elevation of the global water mass. While in the 1990s satellites seemed to promise to render coastal tide gauges obsolete, recent decades have revealed their continued necessity. At a certain point satellites suggested slower than expected sea level  rise. This was due to a mere calibration error, that could only be detected  and corrected through comparison with long-term tide gauge records. Local measurements, frequently perceived as 'messy', turned out to be precisely what was needed to validate global observations.

The history of the humble tide gauge is a story of slow and overlapping technological advancements, that shows the resilience of infrastructure in a measurement process that has become increasingly crucial in a time of radical global change as our own. Over more than three centuries, the physical appearance of the measuring infrastructure has remained largely unchanged: the basic principle of the stilling well persists, while major advances have been made as regards the technological backend.
