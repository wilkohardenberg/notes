## Action — Histories of Planning

Knowledge production is necessarily dynamic. A polyphony of actions at a variety of scales, from the individual to the species, from the local to the global is, in fact, the framework within which knowledge is made. An attention to the role of action allows us thus go beyond the end products to explore the role of multiple agencies in giving shape to what is known and how it is expressed. Rather obviously, considering this intrinsic focus on the transience of knowledge, particular attention has to be paid to processes of change, mediation, and conflict and their role in its planning and social production. Similarly, considering knowledge production as a process and an ensemble of actions forces us to focus on how lacks in knowledge or uncertainties are handled, for example through approximation, inference, or estimation.

At the core of our interests lies thus in the way in which decisions and assessments are made in the moment on the basis of partial knowledge and how these acts are to be seen as stepping stones in a broader process of knowledge production.

### New

Baselines  used as benchmarks for quantitative modelling and forecasting are not  fixed indices of a more ecologically robust past. Instead, they are  shifting artifacts of cultural imagination, debates over historical  memory, the nexus of power and knowledge, and the distributed agency of  human, non-human, and post-human actors operating at multiple  geographic and temporal scales. Their choice, as that of other criteria  and measurements used to assess change, is clearly a matter of  judgement. Different societies adopt different metrics and reference  points and within such societies different interests groups push towards specific definitions of baselines and frameworks. This, obviously,  influences the way societies respond to change. Building on this idea,  the Art of Judgement research cluster explores the individual actions,  communal practices, and social processes through which human societies  keep track of changes in the natural world and react to them. Histories  of anthropogenic change have tended to focus each on a specific aspect  of the complex ensemble of relationships that we will call here, for the sake of simplicity, Anthropocene: stratigraphy, climate change,  biodiversity loss, or the role of capitalism. Moving the attention to  the means by which such change is measured and apprehended the cluster  overcomes such disciplinary cleavages and offers an integrated reading  of the issue as a whole. A better understanding of present apprehensions of global change, as well as historical narratives, is, in fact,  inevitably dependent on the detailed mapping of the conceptual and  material roots of the tools and theories used to measure it. In this  sense this cluster is part of a wider, global debate in recent scholarship about the need to join history of science with the methods  and techniques of environmental history.
