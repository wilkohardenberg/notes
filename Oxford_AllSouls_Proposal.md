**The Level of the Sea. A Conceptual History**

The level of the sea has become, in recent decades, a trope of environmental discourse, used widely to symbolise current and future changes to the environment. The scientific and technological means used to measure, assess, and interpret these changes have however a history that spans a much longer period than that covered by the actual awareness of the role of humans in modifying the Earth’s environment. While mean sea level has thus essentially become naturalized as the standard vertical datum and the reference point for the relationship between land and sea, in our day to day lives we tend to forget that it is a product of specific historically and culturally determined assumptions. Seemingly promising global reach and absolute stability in separating land and sea, in reality its name covers and conflates a number of competing, local vertical datums, the products of peculiar national histories, political factors and environmental conditions. 

Following upon these premises, the broader book project as part of which I plan to come to Oxford looks at the history of an understudied topic: how has the level of the sea been conceptualized theoretically as a vertical datum since early modern times to then become the powerful symbol of the Anthropocene it is now. I am interested particularly in how this concept originated within cultural, social, and scientific agendas that were radically different from current ones and was then attributed environmental value. In doing so I plan to uncover an exemplary aspect of the conceptual prehistory of the current debates about anthropogenic environmental change. 

In my work I approach sea level from a multiplicity of points of view, in an attempt to provide a comprehensive view of the complexity of its conceptualisation throughout modernity. Central in this work are, for instance, questions related to the standardization and unification of reference frameworks, to how “scientific objects” come into being, to the role of place and space in influencing the historical development of reference systems, and to how the materiality of measurement has affected the idea of sea level.

Looking at sea level has led me to explore multiple aspects of science that do not often end up being considered together, ranging from the strictly practical to the highly theoretical. Starting from early modern debates about diluvialism and the theory of the diminution of the sea and the rise in the 19th century of the Ice Age theory, my research looks in detail also at more hands-on activities such as geodesy and engineering, which played a crucial role in making the infrastructural turn of the 19th century possible and were hugely influenced by reasons of national pride and imperial control. My work highlights the connections between these different strands as to provide us with a more wholesome understanding of the theoretical and material origins of one of the most cited reference points of our times. 

Until now my research has mostly focussed on continental Europe, looking in particular at the earliest measurements of standard sea level in Venice, Sweden, and the Netherlands, the scholarly debates about uniformation of vertical datums held by the ancestor organisations of the International Union of Geodesy and Geophysics, the earliest formalisation of the ideas that led to the devising of the geoid, and the role of geopolitics in the conceptualisation of  sea level. I plan to dedicate a stay in Oxford to expand my understanding of how these same issues parallelly developed in Great Britain, where the idea of a mean tide level was apparently first formalised by William Whewell.[^1] 

In particular I am interested in exploring the archives of the British Association for the Advancement of Science at Bodleian Library in the hope to find material on the role of the Association in fostering the definition of mean sea level and its setting as the standard vertical datum.[^3] Furthermore I plan to go through the papers of Oxford's first readers in geology, William Buckland and John Phillips at the archive of the Oxford University Museum of Natural History, on the outlook for links between geodetical theorization and broader geological thinking, in particular in regard to the development of the Ice Age theory.[^2] Finally, I think that also the extensive collections of barometers and surveying instruments held at the History of Science Museum may provide some productive insights on the developing materiality of the measurement and use of vertical datums.

Furthermore, as my project aims to bring together history of science and environmental history, looking at the conjunction between material cultures, environmental contexts, and science, I hope to make good use of the debate and discussion opportunities offered by the existing communities in both fields existing in Oxford, such as the Centre for History of Science, Medicine and Technology, the Centre for the History and Philosophy of Physics, and the Oxford Environmental History Network. I also hope to be able to share some insights about the complex history of such a fleeting and changing concept with some of the many scientists working in Oxford on sea level rise and climate change, such as, for instance, those convening in the Oxford Water Network.

The planned output of my stay is thus to add to my manuscript much needed material on the British contributions to how sea level has been perceived and understood from the 18th to the 21st century. The gathered material will flow into different sections of the book.

[^1]: William Whewell 1794-1866., ‘Researches on the Tides, Seventh Series: On the Diurnal Inequality of the Height of the Tide Especially at Plymouth and at Singapore and on the Mean Level of the Sea’, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society*, 1837, 75–85.
[^2]: See Bert Hansen, ‘The Early History of Glacial Theory in British Geology’, *Journal of Glaciology* 9, no. 55 (1970): 135–41.
[^3]: Michael S. Reidy, *Tides of History: Ocean Science and Her Majesty’s Navy* (University Of Chicago Press, 2008), 139.
