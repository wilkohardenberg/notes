### Seeing Sea Level from Above: A Recent History 

Precariousness is intrinsic to all sea level measurements, as they depend on the constant changes experienced by the sea. Nonetheless, mean sea level is routinely used as a baseline for a variety of measures, including altitude measurements; in most countries the official height reference framework refers to sea level in some way. The idea of sea level as a benchmark for elevations has by now been around for so long as to go essentially unnoticed—we mention it without pausing to consider what it means. That the concept has a history is easily ignored. We tend to forget that sea level is—far from a natural index—a product of technically and culturally determined assumptions. 

Establishing the level of the sea is part of a broader striving to reform and unify reference points and units of measure that has taken place since early modernity. The definition of the meter, the choice of a prime meridian, and the standardization of time are examples of this process. The use of sea level both as a baseline for measuring change and as a reference point for altitude is intertwined with a long-held perception of holocenic stability. And, paradoxically, with its recent upheaval, as climate change introduces new instability to our bases.

The first time an altitude referred to sea level was recorded on a map seems to have been in 1712. It was a product of a new technological device (the barometer) and a cultural push in the sciences towards ever greater precision. Before, across the globe, altitudes were measured without a standard reference point, if at all. Most commonly it was a relative height in respect to the lowest perceived point on earth, when not just valley bottom. But also a lake would work, or a church's pavement, or if it was close by indeed the sea. Frequently they weren't measured at all: statements were just made about which mountain was higher than others (Mawangdui, bars). The understanding of height was different in pre-modern times (effort instead of metrication; Andes). usually the otherness and danger of mountains was foregrounded, over their physical height. Caves and passes, as places of interchange were deemed more important. Thus also the standardization of units of measure contributed in the outlook for a standard reference point for heights. 

In Europe since the 18th century mathematization made exact measurements ever more important. In parallel, the rise of mountaineering as a sport and physical challenge strengthened the need and desire to know elevations, set records.

Over the course of the 17th and 18th century the perceived dropping level of the Baltic sea had led to the generalization that sea level was sinking on a global scale. This fit nicely with a post-Diluvian, religiously informed narrative, but had no definitive answer to the question: where does the water end up. Theories for this included subterranean resevoirs and the gradual loss of water into space. At the turn of the 19th century geologists started to see more clearly the discrepancies in regional accounts: if the Baltic was sinking, the North sea along the Dutch coast seemed to be rising as did the Adriatic. Accounts from colonial possessions also begun to complicate the matter. But, on the macroscale, water, for its physical features necessarily rises or drops globally. One sea can't be at a lower altitude than another and water masses can't move vertically in opposite directions. So the idea started to develop that sea level and the actual mass of water were stable globally, while the recorded differences were the consequence in local shifts of the earth's crust. A growing consensus around the idea of a stable sea made its level increasingly more viable as a common reference plain for altitude measurements. 

We weren't however yet at a coherent definition of what level ought to be used for this purpose, and different sea levels were used for a time for this purpose before ways to calculate a "mean sea level" were devised around the 1830s, when half-tide was increasinggly standardized as such.

The first self-recording tide gauge we know of was installed 1831 at Sheerness Royal Dock in Great Britain. This was part of a longer process of mechanization of measurement. The use of floating devices in stilling wells as means to reduce the disturbance of sea movements on measurement was introduced almost two centuries earlier. The innovation introduced in the early 19th century was relatively minor: clockwork, a rotating drum and a pen were added to the existing model of gauge, allowing to draw a line on paper representing the level of the sea at any given moment over time and to produce thus continuous recordings of sea level. If the technical aspect was minor, the outcome was just short of revolutionary. 

It allowed, in fact, to measure sea level throughout the day for longer periods without human interventions. Previously individual officers needed to be commissioned to read the level at regular, very frequent, intervals. This was a huge effort, that led, as noted by some contemporaries, to made up data points. The mechanization of this process could create more consistent data. Using the words of the ocenaographer David Cartwright it "changed the very concept of tidal variation from a daily sequence of extrema to a continuous process in time." 

The transition was not immediate. The new machines were expensive and by the end of the century most of the stations along Europe's coasts were still set up with manual principles. And there are reports as well of the possibility that mechanical gauges might create false data. In a booklet published in 1931 titled *Marseille, ou la mer qui monte* (Marseille, or the rising sea), Auguste Bouchayer, an entrepreneur and engineer from Grenoble, reports, for instance, on his hunt for the technical means by which the mean level of the Mediterranean at the port of Marseille is determined. When Bouchayer arrives at the automated tide gauge, or “integrator,” that was set up in 1884 in the city’s harbor, he realizes in horror that the data it’s collected over almost fifty years has been corrupted. The wife of the gauge’s warden, looking after the machinery while her husband is out fishing, reveals to Bouchayer and his companion—Louis Le Doyen, an amateur archaeologist working at the city’s roads and water administration—that when the sea is too strong, they just close the lower basin. Then they can sleep without fretting that the float’s cable might break. But by doing so they effectively impede the gauge’s ability to record continuous measurements. Bouchayer expresses his dismay: “My dear Le Doyen, your integrator, marvel of mechanics, is rigged! Our bases are precarious!”

This slow transition however stabilised the premises for the measurement of mean sea level at different locations along the coast at the same time. And thus the possibility to actually use it as a transnational standard. In the following decades, the availability of continuous data allowed to reframe half-tide into actual mean sea level, as it provided the possibility to average all individual data points rather than just the extremes, providing greater accuracy.

No historical path is predetermined. As for so many human ventures, different proposals were made for how to plan sea level measurements from early on. Alexander Humboldt had, for instance, proposed in the 1840s that measurement of sea level should be taken at select locations around the world and compared at regular time intervals, as to track if there were long term changes in the global level of the sea. What occured howevere in the following decades was that where and when tide gauges should be installed was not selected on the basis of a plan, but rather haphazardly following the growth of European colonial possessions (India) and commercial interests (Suez, Panama). This uncoordinated diffusion favoured some areas of the globe over others, creating the preconditions for a skewed view of how sea level varies.

The material bases for standardizing the assessment of elevations were thus both directed and limited by national politics, colonial efforts, and international competition. French authorities kept the estimated sea level they originally chose as their national datum, while the British repeatedly changed theirs to reflect technological advancements. Both were political decisions; neither favored a more “natural” baseline or evinced a more scientific method. The resistance to change and innovation in the case of the Suez canal (where old ideas of a radical difference in height between the Red Sea and the Mediterranean, were initially supported by neasurements made by the French military) and the continual interagency conflict in British India were likewise products of particular political conditions. Similarly, attempts to attain an *international* reference plane had to compete with existing national ones by demonstrating some added value. Surveyors were employees of the state, and for most of them propagandizing the virtues and advantages of national benchmarks amounted to a natural defense of their corporate interests.

Tracing the history of the search for an international point zero reveals a steadily growing acceptance of the conventional and customary nature of datums. Year after year more and more geodesists accepted the fact that the tridimensional grids they were setting up to measure the earth were useful abstractions, not reflections of a “natural order.” They were the products of politics even more than measurement.

A few things radically changed the way sea level was perceived over the course of the 20th century: first its variability over historical times was accepted and proven, then its actual rise due to climate change was started to be registered. In particular, an increasing number of international projects collecting and comparing sea-level and climate data, along with advances in satellite technology and computation, revolutionized both marine and terrestrial geodesy. This had a major impact on how the reference frame for elevations is defined. In particular, the development of space geodesy allowed practitioners to rethink how to assess heights on land and how measurements made at distant points—in both space and time—are related. In a sense, satellites have made real the idea that mean sea level can be global. Enough data can now be assembled to compute averages on a planetary scale.

Space technology has radically changed the way we perceive our planet. Photographs taken from space have for instance famously contributed to the rise of the idea that the human species has only one world. The *Earthrise* picture, taken by members of the Apollo 8 mission in 1968, reached an iconic status in this regard. The change in perspective primed by the Space Race affected also the framing and understanding of what sea level is. Remote sensing has, in this sense, played a crucial role in adding further layers of meaning to this understanding. It is not only a matter of being able to scale up and move our point of view from the local to the planetary, from the immediate to the mediate, from the biased to the allegedly neutral. It is also an issue of how these different dimensions necessarily coexist in any given moment.

Since the first Sputnik was launched by the Soviet Union on October 4, 1957, satellites played a central role in bringing forward the determination of the shape of Earth. Their deployment affected in particular the redefinition of the relationship between the geoid and the ellipsoid. Jerome D. Rosenberg, the director of NASA’s geodetic satellites program, allegedly stated that, since the launch of the Sputnik, “our knowledge of the form and gravitational field of the earth has increased more […] than during the previous 200 years.” Almost immediately data gathered by specialized satellites were used as proxies for geodetic measurements, primarily through the observation of the “perturbations in [their] movement.” Very detailed lists of the often contradictory requisites for explicitly geodetic satellites were soon published and, beginning as early as 1961, a few experimental satellites dedicated to geodetic tasks were launched. In 1958, the same year the United States put into orbit their own first satellite, multiple articles were published analyzing the Sputnik launch and discussing the possibility of using satellites to refine observations of the planet’s gravity field and thus of the geoid. As early as 1966 the geophysicist William Kaula, dubbed “the father of space-based geodesy”, published his seminal *Theory of Satellite Geodesy*, which included a detailed preliminary analysis of artificial satellites’ orbit perturbations.

On the quest for methods to broaden a burgeoning global database of gravity measurements, geophysicists had come across another community of scientists on the lookout instead for practical issues in which to apply the techniques they were developing. The newly-minted experts in satellite remote sensing were eager to test the applied prospects of the recent techno-scientific breakthrough. From the very beginning satellites showed potential to overcome the undersampling and underestimation of temporal changes that had characterized earlier decades, as well as the vagaries of geopolitical control caused by warm and cold wars. Giff Ewing, a US American pioneer in the field, noted how, in the years following the International Geophysical Year (IGY) in 1957–1958 oceanographers had increasingly come to appreciate the difficulties inherent in averaging height and gravity measurements between stations set up mainly for the needs of the land survey and located at great distance in both space and time. The chance to collect data beyond the limited number of existing stations, reaching from orbit to the high sea, promised to revolutionize the way in which sea levels were conceived.

Between the late 1960s and the early 1970s, both the United States and the Soviet Union actively pursued ways to improve marine geodesy through the means of satellite technology. This was part of a generalized push towards further improving of geodetic methods and techniques. Besides discussing the potential of setting up a system of high satellites dedicated to determining coordinates—which about ten years later would become GLONASS, the Soviet satellite navigation system—Soviet geodesists envisioned the deployment of low altitude satellites, able to investigate the gravitational field on the basis of variations in the satellites’ movements. This would allow for further refinement of the data collected by radio altimeter about the height of satellites above water surface, which at the time had a precision of a few meters. The combination with satellite altimetry would allow researchers, it was the hope, to immediately improve this to just one meter.

Satellites offered the possibility for overcoming the strict limitations of collecting gravitational observations at sea by boat. The method seemed, in fact, to be particularly apt for studying the shape of the geoid, as it would enable to assess the intensity of gravity directly on the surface of the sea, revolutionizing from the onset old approaches to the matter. For instance, research on oceanographic topography has been revolutionized by the implementation of satellites. In the early 1980s the accuracy of gravimetric measurements was further improved by combining them more strictly with altimetric observations. The products were, however, general mappings of the geoid—that is rough estimates of where the surface of the sea ought to be—rather than detailed investigations of water levels. The resolution was not yet detailed enough to allow more specific analyses. The 1980s were marked, at least in the United States, by a push towards an improvement of the understanding of the sea, justified by its possible economic and military uses.

From 1993 advances in satellite technology allowed then, with the launch of the French-American TOPEX/POSEIDON satellite, the first dedicated oceanography satellite, to produce a steady planetary monitoring of global mean sea level from orbit. This made it possible to address the historical bias of tide gauge observations. In more recent years the work of this satellite has been complemented by NASA’s Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) and ESA’s Gravity field and steady-state Ocean Circulation Explorer (GOCE) projects, which combine altimetry and long-wavelength gravimetry. Obviously, as has always been the case, all these developments in the ways in which sea level was monitored affected exclusively potential future data. Any analysis necessarily had to take into account the intrinsic biases of past measurements, as well as the technological and methodological developments. The shift in the early 1990s from tide gauges to satellites as the primary means to record global mean sea level change has been, for instance, called upon by climate skeptics as a reason to doubt the scientists’ ability to produce significant comparisons of the rate of change in the two periods. In other words, skeptics wonder if the increased rate of change since the 1990s is a product of the tools used to measure sea level rise, rather than the recording of an actual geophysical phenomenon. In truth, tide gauge data have been recorded, quite obviously, also in the years following the launch of TOPEX/POSEIDON. A comparison of the two parallel series shows, if anything, that between 1993 and 2013 satellite altimetry has produced lower estimates of global mean sea level change than tide gauges.

Satellite oceanography, in both its altimetric and gravimetric components, apparently offers the possibility of achieving the dream of absolute precision, objectivity, and scientific neutrality in determining sea levels that marked earlier attempts at this endeavor and decades’ long international scientific debates. In about twenty years, since the launch of the GEOS-3 satellite in 1975 until TOPEX/POSEIDON became operative in 1993, alleged accuracy in measurements has improved more than ten-fold. Nowadays satellites allow us to establish the height of the sea with unprecedented accuracy. Satellites have allowed scientists to overcome the limits of a sparse network of coastal and mid-ocean island tide gauges and gravimeters, finally supplying continuous—and nearly global—sea level data. The estimated average margin of error of the most recently deployed oceanographic satellite, OSTM/JASON-2, is expected to be just 3.4 cm. In the current century a new generation of satellites combining geodetic and oceanographic observations seems to have opened new pathways to the redefinition of existing height reference systems.

Nonetheless, the impression provided by satellites that absolute precision of measurement could be reached—especially in respect to the perceived messiness of local observations—has been disproved in recent years. Only due to the possibility of comparing satellite data with historical series produced at tide gauges could researchers fix an error in the calibration of the instruments that was producing the impression of a slower than forecast trend in sea level rise. The usefulness and significance of the coastal measurements so criticized by German geodesists in the nineteenth century appears vindicated. In fact, valid, long-term series of observations reliably collected on the ground are a prime necessity for calibrating the instruments deployed on satellites. As noted by Höhler and Wormbs, by increasing the need for sensitive instruments, distance has increased their complexity and proneness to error. 

Just like the men and officials delegated to laboriously observing sea level fluctuations on the simplest tide gauges along the coasts of Europe in the early nineteenth century, the hardware and software used to collect and assess measurements in the twenty-first can be subject to drifts and errors with significant consequences. Satellites make it possible to get a sense of global sea level thanks to the amount of observations that they can collect and to their ability to cover the whole globe without apparently favoring one area in respect to another. But in reality they remain dependent on the availability of local observations and long-term data series. Their history is also interwoven with that of Cold War surveillance. As noted by many this may negatively affect their perceived neutrality and objectivity. The choice of the instruments deployed on satellites, determines, as noted again by Höhler and Wormbs, what is recorded: strategical and tactical decisions have played crucial roles in how satellite altimetry and gravimetry developed since the launch of the first Sputnik.

In parallel to a steady improvement in satellite technologies, earth scientists in the late twentieth century developed new ways of looking at change. And in this context sea level became an ever more relevant baseline for evaluating global anthropogenic changes. By 1975 the possibility that the recorded trend of atmospheric cooling might be reversed due to increased emissions of carbon dioxide had begun to register with climatologists. The geochemist Wallace Broecker, in what is said to have been the first article to use the phrase “global warming,” noted the extreme likelihood that anthropogenic climate change would affect the level of the sea in unexpected ways. This idea had immediate success and bolstered research on the connections between increases in global sea level and in mean global temperature.

Satellites have made it possible, after centuries of debates about its viability as a standard, to abandon sea level as a reference plane and look for other ways to assess heights and altitudes. Since the late 1970s national survey agencies and international science consortia have developed new reference planes of very different kinds. Some have focused on the geoid, emphasizing direct measurements of the gravitational field. Others went back to broad mathematical generalizations, such as the ellipsoid adopted by the World Geodetic System in 1984. In a way this has freed up the great amount of data collected over more than a century and a half to become the basis of our measurements in sea level change, rather than the baseline for altitudes. Mean sea level is still a baseline, just of something completely different: the gradual climate catastrophe through which we are living rather than the description of the stable landscape we thought we were living on.

Over the course of the 1980s and 1990s the connection between climate change and sea level rise became common knowledge, discussed well beyond scientific circles, including representatives of the Reagan administration and a number of government offices around the world. Conversely this then further bolstered international research on the issue and then the institution of the IPCC. Beyond technology and science, there is, thus, another thing that has revolutionized how we understand sea level: risk! Sea-level rise is transforming human relationship to coastal locations, making life more difficult while destroying long held ideas of Holocenic stability and ciclicity, that is the possibility to recognise weather patterns and the assurance that prime locations will preserve their qualities in the foreseeable future. 

One thing has to be stated clearly: for all its limitations, including its embeddedness in colonial epistemologies and intangibility and immateriality, mean sea level is still a useful metric that has served humanity well, acting over time as both a baseline for elevation measurements and a powerful symbol of anthropogenic global change. But it is not the only possible way to visualize and understand sea level. In what might seem a drastically disjunctive interpretation, the Māori people of Aotearoa/New Zealand, for instance, don’t see land and sea as in binary opposition, but rather as parts of a continuity that can only be understood holistically. Accordingly there is no need for sea levels as they were presented by colonial geodesists. Sea level as a concept is a product of Western ontologies. But even within Western science, a true comprehension of how sea and land interact increasingly requires a multiplicity of reference points on a variety of spatial and temporal scales. Oceanographers, for instance, model the geoid as stable and refer to the variability of the seas’ surface height—that is, the differences between the geoid and the recorded state of water—in respect to fixed points. While this is true in the short term assuming such a stability offers clear advantages, ongoing climate change through sea-level rise will also gradually affect the geoid.

The practice of gauging sea levels and the choices we make as a society and as a scientific community about baselines, scales, and metrics still inform the way humanity interprets global changes in the level of the sea. In this book I highlight how the conceptualization of sea level as an average, an approximation, and a neat separator between land and sea, as well as the materiality of its measurement, affects our current understanding of its rise as a gradual process. I also stress how, as with all scientific categories, the choice of which sea level to adopt and use is contingent. A time of change asks for more nuanced interpretations, ones that accept and foster different categories of analysis at different scales. If the public accepts such an understanding of sea level, it might become possible to produce more effective political responses to the threats posed by a global rise in water levels—not because one or the other level is intrinsically better but because each analytic framework is specifically attuned to a particular historical moment or spatially determined condition. The local and the global dimensions of future changes along the oceans’ coasts must always be considered holistically, providing clear and understandable information of how the different scales and systems of reference are connected to each other. Profiling the conceptual historicity of sea level as a baseline and examining its constructedness can contribute to achieving this.
