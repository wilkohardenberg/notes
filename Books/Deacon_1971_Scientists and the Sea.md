263: Whewell took "a leading role in the British Association's work to establish mean sea level"

265: "To compare the rise and fall of the tide from one place to another it became necessary to have a universally accepted point of reference from which to measure it. Engineers and surveyors had been accustomed to regard the land as beginning at the low water mark of the lowest ebbs but this varied from place to plase as much as 20 feet. Was the mean sea level the same everywhere and if so how could it be defined?"

"Variations in sea level do occur, due to density differences, wind stress, currents and other factors but they are generally very small. In the eighteenth and eraly nineteenth centuries when accurate information on the subject was unavailable they were generally either exaggerated or dismissed altogether. John Walker, lecturing at Edinburgh in the 1780s, referred sceptically to the idea that the Pacific Ocean at Panama was some thousands of feet higher than the Atlantic Ocean on the other side of the isthmus.[^walker]"

"The practice of equating mean sea level with half-tide level , the mid-point of the combined range of rise and fall, was apparently adopted during the 1830s by observers such as William Walker at Plymouth and Captain Henry Denham (1800-1887) who made new observations at Liverpool  as part of his hydrographic work on the Lancashire coast. Whewell demonstrated with records from Plymouth and Singapore that this point varied little from day to day. Variations in athmospheric pressure and wind were recognizable factors in producing the differences which did occur."[^delabeche]

[^delabeche]: H.T. de la Beche, "On the influence of atmospheric pressure on the tidal waters of Cornwall and Devon", _Edinburgh New Philosophical Journal_, (1838-1839). Vol. 26, pp. 415-19

266: "It remained to show that tha half-tide level was the same everywhere before adopting it as certain. The BA had virst voted money for the project in 1834 but it was not until 1837-38 that the work was actually done. Levelling was carried out between three points on the coast in the west of England and tidal observations made. The results showed satisfactorily that the mean tide level provided a consistent norm for mean sea level and that the two could be considered as interchangeable."[^whewell]

[^whewell]: William Whewell "Account of a level line, measured from the British Channel to the English Channel, during the year 1837-38, by Mr. Bunt", _Report on the eighth meeting of the British Association for the Advancement of Science, 1838_ (1839). Part 1, pp. 1-11; [@whewell_researches_1839].

The work was carried out by T.G. Bunt an enthusiastic student of tides, who in 1836 had set up a self-registering tide gauge in Bristol by his own designs. Whewell got the BA to pay him grants.
